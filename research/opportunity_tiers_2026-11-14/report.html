Receiver Breakout Equations

What changes for a receiver against a top-5 or bottom-5 pass defense, what turns a normal 50–60 yard receiver into an 80–100 yard game, and when his catches go up. Every input is something known before kickoff; the in-game tables show what the breakout games looked like after the fact.

Bottom-5 vs top-5 defense, pre-game±4–6%

That is the yards swing for the same receiver. The defense changes yards per target, not targets. Even against the worst defenses, a 52-yard receiver projects to about 54.

What an 80+ game takes+3 targets and a 30+ catch

With both, normal receivers hit 80 in 88% of games. With neither, 3.5%. Targets averaged 9.5 in their 80+ games against 6.6 normally.

Catches go up withTargets × catch rate

Catches track targets almost one for one. Catches fall about 1.6% for each extra yard of average depth of target (aDOT); catch rate runs from 71% for short receivers to 60% for deep ones. Implied team total adds about 0.9% per point.

Real defense quality matters about one for one. Measured on full-season numbers, a defense that allows 10% more yards per attempt gives a receiver about 9% more yards (elasticity 0.91 ± 0.16). It has no effect on his targets (−0.01 ± 0.11).

Pre-game ranks catch only a slice of that. Only 61% of the defenses ranked bottom 5 going into a game finished the season there, and 57% of the top 5. The pre-game position-specific measure (yards allowed to WRs or TEs) carries an elasticity of 0.18. The ranks firm up after Week 8, when the yards gap between top-5 and bottom-5 defenses grows from 5% to 14%.

The tier does not move the 80+ rate. Normal receivers hit 80 in 20–24% of games in every tier. A bad defense raises the average a little and makes almost no difference to the breakout chance.

The equations

Fit on 2022–24 with a log link, so every term is a percentage multiplier. Tested on 2025–26: the yards equation correlates 0.530 with what happened, against 0.507 for the player's season average; the receptions equation scores 0.577.

Targets (T) = 0.6 × his targets per game + 0.4 × (last-3 target share × team targets per game) YPT = his yards per target, shrunk toward 8.0 by 30 targets D = yards the defense allows to his position per game ÷ league average Yards = 1.176 × T^1.069 × YPT^0.828 × D^0.181 × e^(0.0090 × (implied − 22)) Receptions = 0.579 × T^1.035 × D^0.099 × e^(−0.0159 × (aDOT − 9)) × e^(0.0085 × (implied − 22)) P(80+) = 1 / (1 + e^−(−10.851 + 2.401 × ln Yards + 0.0297 × (aDOT − 9))) P(100+) = 1 / (1 + e^−(−12.792 + 2.681 × ln Yards + 0.0357 × (aDOT − 9)))

How to read the exponents. Targets carry more than 1-for-1 (1.07): 10% more targets gives 10.7% more yards. Efficiency carries 0.83, because a high yards-per-target partly regresses. The defense carries 0.18: a defense allowing 24% more yards to WRs than average (a typical bottom 5) adds 4%. Each point of implied team total adds 0.9%, so 28 vs 22 adds 5.5%.

An 80-yard game from a 55-yard receiver is volume plus one big play. The volume comes from the team throwing more than usual (+7%) and from him getting a bigger slice. The big play is the part nobody forecasts: a recent run of big plays does not predict the next one, and in this repo's earlier research neither did a defense's explosive-play rate.

Shootouts help, blowouts don't. His 80+ games ran 7+ points over the total 39% of the time, against 27% otherwise. Trailing by 8+ gave his team the most targets (34.2) but him the fewest yards (48.2) and the lowest 80+ rate (16%). Teams that fall far behind are usually bad passing offenses. Close games were best: 23% hit 80.

Catches are a volume stat with a catch-rate dial. They track projected targets almost one for one (exponent 1.04). The dial is depth: each yard of aDOT costs about 1.6% of catches, so a slot receiver with a 6-yard aDOT catches about 10% more than a 12-yard deep threat on the same targets. Implied total adds 0.85% a point. A bad defense against his position adds a little (exponent 0.10), about 2% for a typical bottom 5.

Small underdogs throw the most. Teams 3–6.5 point underdogs averaged 32.4 targets. Big underdogs throw less (30.4) and their receivers catch the fewest passes. They tend to be weak offenses. Among normal receivers, close games produced the most catches (4.34) and big leads the fewest (3.83).

The reasoning, in order

  1. Start with volume. Projected targets drive everything with an exponent above 1. Blend his season targets with last-3 share times team targets. If that blend is under 7.5 for a 50–60 yard receiver, an 80-yard game needs a big play and is roughly a 1-in-5 shot whatever the matchup.
  2. Look for added volume. The biggest pre-game lift is a teammate out: 20%+ of the team's target share missing raised the remaining receivers' targets about 15% against games with nobody missing. Next are a rising last-3 share, a game total of 45+, and a small-underdog script.
  3. Then the defense, mostly for efficiency. A bottom-5 defense adds about 4–6% to yards and about 2% to catches, through yards per target, not targets. Trust the ranks more after Week 8. Deep receivers gain most against bad defenses; short receivers barely move.
  4. The team total. 0.9% more yards and catches per point of implied total. It matters most combined with volume: a 9.5+ target receiver at 28+ implied hit 80 in 62% of games, against 39% at 20–24.
  5. Price the big play, don't predict it. 61% of breakouts had a 30+ catch, and nothing pre-game reliably says which week it comes. For yards overs on 50–60 yard receivers, the edge has to come from the number, not a feeling about the matchup. Catches are steadier because they depend on targets and depth, not on one long gain.
  6. For catches, favor short-aDOT receivers with rising targets in close games or as small underdogs. Avoid deep threats and big underdogs.
