Overnight research · Week 10, 2026

Can we see a boom game coming?

Five questions from Friday night, tested on every receiver game from 2022 to 2026 (13,526 games), using only what was known before each game, then applied to Sunday's games. Research only.

Explosive gamesPartly. Volume, efficiency and the implied team total pick out who is likelier to boom. A hot streak of big plays does not.
Seesaw pairsNo. A team's top two receivers do not trade off. They boom together more often than apart.
QB changesRarely. Shares move about 2.5 points; reweighting to the new QB's few games makes projections worse.
"Defense allows a lot to TEs"Not more targets. The offense's own target tree decides who gets the ball. The defense mostly changes yards per catch.
Rams favored without starsExplained. A big team-quality gap, Arizona's quarterback situation, and a low total that already prices the missing receivers.

1 · Can you predict a receiver exploding?

You can rank who is more likely to boom, but even the best candidates hit 100 yards about 1 game in 5. Recent big plays add nothing.

  • Predicts it: targets per game and target share (the strongest by far), yards per target, and the implied team total. Each extra point of implied team total raises the odds of a 100-yard game by about 4.5%, so a team implied at 28 points carries about 40% higher odds than one at 20.
  • Does not: big plays in his last 2 or 3 games (the "hot hand" coefficient is about zero, p = 0.76), what the defense allowed on explosive plays (p = 0.27), or wind.
  • Barely: a receiver's deep profile (target depth, deep-target rate) improves the 30+ catch forecast a hair (Brier 0.1017 to 0.1016) and not the 100-yard forecast.
  • Tested on seasons the models never saw: the top tenth of predictions said 22.6% for 100+ yards and 21.6% happened; for a 30+ yard catch, 34.0% predicted, 32.6% happened.

What this means for the board: the opportunity simulation already uses the parts that work (volume, efficiency, the spread and total). Treat a recent run of long catches as noise, not a signal.

2 · Do receiver pairs seesaw?

No. Across 1,922 games of a team's top two receivers, their yards move slightly together (correlation +0.06), not opposite.

  • When the WR1 has 100+ yards, the WR2 also reaches 100 8.9% of the time, more than his usual 6.2%. Big passing days lift both.
  • When the WR2 is held under 40, the WR1 still reaches 100 only 17.2% of the time, the same as usual (17.5%). Locking one down does not feed the other.
  • Which one wins the targets in a given game follows their season shares (correlation 0.32), with lots of game-to-game noise. Nothing in the free pregame data reliably calls the flip.

Green Bay is the clearest example of "together": weeks 5 and 9 both went over 100. Cincinnati shows real flips (week 4: Higgins 157, Chase 27; week 9: Chase 155, Higgins 32), but they don't line up with anything knowable before kickoff.

3 · Vikings at Packers: does Week 1 repeat?

A blow-up in the first meeting does not carry to the rematch.

  • Across 1,380 same-season rematches, how far a player beat his average in the first game says almost nothing about the second (correlation +0.035). Players with 100+ in the first meeting averaged 62 yards in the rematch, close to their usual 68.
  • Week 1 also had Carson Wentz at quarterback for Minnesota; Kirk Cousins is listed now.

4 · Quarterback changes: Nabers and Likely

QB changes move target shares less than they feel like, and a few games with the new QB are too few to trust.

  • In 1,548 games where the starter differed from the usual one, shares moved 2.5 points on average. Predicting targets from the season share missed by 1.74 targets a game; using only games with the new QB missed by 1.83. The full-season share was better.
  • The Giants so far: under Winston (weeks 2-6) Likely drew 24-41% of targets; in McCarthy's two games in this data he drew 15% and 22%, while Nabers stayed at 30-33%. That is the opposite of "McCarthy feeds Likely underneath", but two games prove nothing either way, and Thursday's game is not in the play-by-play yet.

5 · "They give up a lot to tight ends": is the opportunity there?

A defense that allows a lot to a position does not send more targets there. The offense's own habits decide who gets the ball.

  • League-wide (2022-2026): a defense that sees 20% more tight-end targets than average moved the offense's tight-end share by +0.10 points (p = 0.64); for receivers -0.09 (p = 0.84). Only backs show a small real effect (+0.74 points, p < 0.001).
  • Detroit allows the most to tight ends: 10.8 targets, 8.0 catches and 88 yards a game (league 7.6, 5.4, 55). That raises what Hunter Henry does per target, not how many he gets. Drake Maye spreads the ball: Henry's share has ranged from 7% to 24%.

6 · How the Bills spread the ball

Your read is right: Buffalo's targets are among the least predictable in the league. Allen throws to whoever the play frees up, so no receiver is a steady weekly volume bet.

  • Buffalo's correlation is 0.17 (Seattle 0.91); New England's is -0.08.
  • In 8 games, Shakir's share rose against defenses that played more man coverage last season (correlation +0.58) and Moore's fell (-0.23). Tested across 2023-2025, short receivers do not reliably gain share against man coverage (p = 0.53), so treat Buffalo's pattern as noise until it repeats.
  • The steady piece is James Cook: 22.3 projected carries and a 95-yard rushing median this week. Each Bills receiver projects a median between 23 and 35 yards.

7 · Rams at Cardinals: why the Rams are big favorites

The market has the Rams by 9.5 with a total of just 39.5. The low total is the market saying it knows the Rams' receivers are out. The spread says the rest of the matchup is lopsided.

What changes on the board

  • Nothing that failed these tests gets added: no "hot hand", no seesaw adjustment, no QB-only shares, no extra targets from what a defense allows a position.
  • Worth adding, because it held up: a 100+ yard chance and a 30+ yard catch chance on each player card, built from volume, efficiency and the implied team total, and a target-tree steadiness note for teams like Buffalo and New England, so a soft matchup isn't mistaken for guaranteed volume.

Method: logistic models fit leaving one season out (2022-2026); Brier scores and calibration by decile; clustered standard errors by game; nflverse play-by-play, rosters and schedules, NGS participation for coverage (through 2025).
